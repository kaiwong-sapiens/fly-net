How the fly plays

Each paddle movement is the end of a chain of spikes through real wiring. The chain below is the fly's visual pursuit pathway, the one males use to chase a moving female during courtship.

Watch how it plays. While the ball is roughly in front, the fly holds still. Once the ball drifts more than about 40° to one side, which happens late in its approach, the fly swings toward it. That style comes straight out of the wiring: the central relay barely reaches the steering neurons, and the peripheral relay drives them hard.

1 · EYES LC10a 234

Visual projection neurons that respond to small moving objects. The ball excites the ones whose receptive fields it falls in, more of them as it gets closer and bigger.

2 · RELAY AOTU019 · AOTU025 4

Two parallel pursuit pathways in the anterior optic tubercle, one for the centre of view and one for the periphery.

3 · NETWORK everything in reach 1,128

Every other neuron this input can make fire, excitatory and inhibitory, simulated spike by spike.

4 · STEERING DNa02 · DNa01 4

Descending neurons whose right-minus-left difference predicts turning in walking flies.

5 · PADDLE turn → speed 0.045

Paddle speed in field widths per second, per spike/s of right-minus-left steering. Nothing else moves it.

Taken from the data

  • Which neuron connects to which, and with how many synapses (FlyWire release 783).
  • Whether each connection excites or inhibits, from predicted neurotransmitters.
  • The cell types and the pursuit pathway LC10a → AOTU019/AOTU025 → DNa02.
  • Where each LC10a looks. Its input neurons place it on the eye's map, and its preference for AOTU019 (central view) over AOTU025 (periphery) orients that map. The two agree at r = 0.90 (right eye) and 0.93 (left eye).

Our modelling choices

  • Every neuron is the same leaky integrate-and-fire unit, with the parameters of Shiu et al. 2024.
  • The ball drives LC10a through Gaussian receptive fields 16° wide. Each eye is assumed to see from 15° across the midline to 150° to its side.
  • The steering-to-paddle gain and the 25 ms smoothing of steering rates.
  • The learning rule and which synapses may change.
  • Arousal is an input gain. In courting males, P1 neurons raise LC10a gain, but P1 is male-specific and this is a female brain.

How training works

The 10,866 synapses that LC10a neurons make onto the rest of the circuit can change. Each keeps an eligibility trace, a fading record (time constant 0.8 s) of moments when the neurons on both sides of it fired together. Dopamine turns that record into change: sugar strengthens recently active synapses and heat weakens them, up to three times or down to zero their wired strength. This three-factor rule is how the fly's mushroom body learns. Applying it to the pursuit pathway is our assumption.

Rally reward sounds like the obvious way to teach Pong, and it barely works. A miss is usually a turn that came too late, and weakening the synapses that were active makes the next turn weaker still. The coach gives feedback every 10 ms instead, the way heat was applied in the classic flight-simulator experiments where tethered flies learned to steer. In our tests, two minutes of coaching took a naive fly from 36% of balls returned to 84%, as good as a courting fly (80%). We measured each over three five-minute runs, with learning switched off for the trained fly.

Checks we ran

  • Simulator vs the published model. Stimulating sugar-sensing neurons, our firing rates match the original Brian2 model at r = 0.999, mean difference 0.6 spikes/s. To get there we had to copy one Brian2 detail: input that arrives during a neuron's 2.2 ms refractory period is discarded.
  • Sub-circuit vs the whole brain. On six random ball trajectories with random synapse strengths, the 1,370-neuron circuit in this page produced spike trains in the steering neurons identical to a simulation of all 138,639 neurons.
  • Pursuit is in the wiring. Within about 30° of straight ahead, the ball drives the central relay AOTU019 while the steering neurons stay nearly silent (under 16 spikes/s). Further out, the peripheral relay AOTU025 joins in and that side's DNa02 fires, reaching 60–110 spikes/s at 90°. The opposite side's DNa02 stayed silent in every condition we tested.
